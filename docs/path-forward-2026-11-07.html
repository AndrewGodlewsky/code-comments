Where Code Comments goes next

You asked for my options and recommendations after the two earlier reviews, the thesis assessment and the Haiku question. This memo puts them together. It covers what we know, how the question has shifted, the paths open to you and which one I would take.

Spend to date: about $15.29 over 778 agent runsBranch: agent/19-downstream-sequence-evalOpen issues: 30
The short answer
  1. Stop tuning the comment skill against small synthetic cases. That eval has done its job. Every arm passes, so it can no longer tell variants apart.
  2. Ship the lean variant as a small hygiene skill now. Its job is to keep comments true and record the non-obvious why. It makes no claims about agent performance. It is cheap, and avoiding harm is the best-supported part of the idea.
  3. Make one bet with the research budget: a long-horizon test of cheap implementers. Haiku builds 6 features in a row on one real repository. It runs plain, with an instruction-only control, with the comments skill, and with a Sonnet reviewer, against a Sonnet ceiling. This tests comments against the alternatives in the setting where you actually feel the pain.
  4. Write the stop rules before running it, cap it at about $60, and let the result decide. If comments don't beat a plain "keep it clean" instruction, stop the efficacy research on comments for good.

The shift

Your question has outgrown the mechanism

The project started with a mechanism: put context in comments so later agents and people make better edits. Over these sessions your real question has come into focus. You want to know what keeps a codebase coherent when agents, especially cheap ones, keep editing it. That is the question behind your three-layer theory, your docs-folder experience and your hesitation about Haiku.

The evidence points to three separate levers. Comments only work through the first.

Knowledge

comments, docs, decision records

Facts the code doesn't show: constraints, what callers rely on, rejected alternatives. This helps when the missing fact is the cause of the mistake.

Judgment

a strong model in the loop

Reuse or rewrite, what to keep, where a change belongs. Most cheap-model churn is a judgment failure. Review and advisor setups supply judgment directly.

Checks

tests, lints, duplication and complexity gates

These turn drift into a failing check instead of a feeling months later. They are cheap, mechanical and free of drift themselves.

This is why my recommendation changed from the thesis assessment. That memo suggested a real-repository test of whether comments help the next reader be correct. Since then, the churn data found something that test would miss. Correctness on a single step has almost no headroom, but unnecessary churn, accumulating over many steps, has plenty. A test should measure where a difference can still show.

Your options

Six paths, and what each would tell you

1. Long-horizon cheap-implementer test

Recommended

Six features in a row on one real repository. Each arm builds on its own earlier work. The arms are Haiku plain, Haiku with a "keep it clean, reuse what exists" instruction, Haiku with the lean comments skill, Haiku with Sonnet review at each step, and Sonnet plain.

Answers
Does Haiku tangle code faster than Sonnet over time? Which lever prevents it? Do comments beat a plain instruction?
Cost
Estimated $25–60 at a hard cap. A one-step smoke run measures real per-session cost first.
Effort
The largest of the six. It needs a container and vulnerability scan, an N-step runner (extends sequence.py), and churn and erosion metrics (extends churn.py).
  • Tests your actual working question, and pays off whatever the result.
  • Gives comments a fair fight against a control they have to beat.
  • No published study covers this for Haiku-class models.
  • Con: it is a pilot, with 1 repository and 2 reps. It shows direction, not proof.

My read: the one experiment whose result changes what you do day to day.

Both sides

Where I might be wrong

Against my recommendation

  • Replays diverge. When an arm's code drifts from the real history, later PR tests may break for reasons unrelated to quality. Public-API grading and dropping invalid steps reduce this, but don't remove it.
  • One repository is one repository. narwhals' backend duplication may favour or penalise comments in ways that don't generalise.
  • Six steps may be too short. Published erosion trends show over 4–8 steps, so a real effect could still be missed.
  • Models move fast. The next Haiku could close the churn gap and make the question moot within months.
  • The build is real work. It takes days of agent time for a directional answer. Parking now is defensible.

For it

  • It measures where the headroom is. Churn and accumulation are where we have already seen a gap. Single-step correctness showed none.
  • The control is fair. An instruction-only arm stops a "the skill helped" result that is really just "telling the model to be careful helped".
  • Most of it exists already. The isolated runner, hand-off, leak guard, no-context control, churn metric, offline regrading and paired analysis are built and tested.
  • Every outcome is useful. Each branch of the decision tree changes something about how you work with cheap models, even if the comments thesis dies.
  • Nobody else has the answer. R5 found no long-horizon study of Haiku-class models.
