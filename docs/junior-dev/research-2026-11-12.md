# Junior-dev: evidence review and eval design

> Produced on 2026-11-12 for [#52](https://github.com/AndrewGodlewsky/code-comments/issues/52) by a research workflow:
> five parallel sweeps (human studies, LLM studies, agent systems, evaluation methods, Claude Code mechanics),
> a skeptical audit of 42 of the 66 load-bearing claims (17 verified, 25 with minor issues that are given here in
> corrected form, none fabricated or misrepresented), and a synthesis. The other 24 claims were not audited and are
> labelled. Raw findings and verdicts: [research-data-2026-11-12.json](research-data-2026-11-12.json). This is a
> targeted review, not a systematic one, and no published result was reproduced locally. The two figures from this
> repository (A6, M1) were rechecked against their source files.

*2026-11-12. From five research sweeps and a citation audit. Statuses: **verified**; **minor issue** (given in corrected form); **unverified** (not audited). Practitioner material is prior art, not evidence. IDs point to section 7.*

## 1. Bottom line

The intent is **untested**. No study supports it directly and no study refutes it directly.

- **The premise is supported.** LLM-drawn architecture diagrams get the components roughly right but the relationships badly wrong, and they hide conflicts behind vague nodes (A1). In people, explaining a mechanism step by step shows the explainer they understand less than they thought (H1).
- **The mechanism only looks plausible when an outside source of truth is involved.** Questions help LLMs when the person answering knows something the asker does not (L7, L8), or when tests or a verifier are in the loop (L2, L3). In Junior-dev the person answering is the plan's author, who may be wrong, and the junior cannot check. This setup, one expert answering a non-expert ("consultancy"), barely beat having no expert at all for LLM judges (L9).
- **"Especially helps weaker models": the evidence leans against it.** Weaker models gained less from self-repair. Haiku 3.5 got as much information from clarification as Sonnet 3.5 but made worse use of it (L3, L7).

**What the evidence does not show:** that explaining a design to a novice improves the design. Human studies measure the explainer's confidence or learning, or how many defects a reviewer finds. In the replications, noticing a gap changed nothing downstream (H2). Nothing shows that the non-adversarial framing matters. In this repo, a review-then-revise arm cost 3.6 times as much per passing step and gave no gain (A6). The repo's existing skill also almost never loads on its own (M1).

A version that holds up has four parts: a questioner with no context, which pins down places the plan leaves unexplained; answers grounded in the repo; those answers written back into the plan; and a test against a self-review on the same budget and a one-line "write it for a junior" instruction.

## 2. What the evidence says

### Human studies

- **H1.** Writing a step-by-step explanation lowered inflated self-rated understanding of mechanisms (devices, natural phenomena). It lowered it much less for facts and not at all for procedures. Rozenblit & Keil 2002. *Verified.*
- **H2.** Preregistered replications found the drop is real but small (about 0.3 on a 7-point scale) and changed no downstream judgments. Listing reasons did not lower understanding and made positions more extreme. Crawford & Ruscio 2021. *Minor issue.*
- **H3.** Self-explanation prompts improve learning (g = .55). Prompts to elaborate concepts (g = .87) beat metacognitive prompts (g = .19, not significant). Bisra et al. 2018. *Minor issue.*
- **H4.** Peer tutors default to restating what they know. In one study, deep tutee questions drew deep answers 41% of the time, against 14% for shallow questions. Tutee questions were infrequent and mostly shallow. Roscoe & Chi 2007. *Minor issue.*
- **H5.** Writing for an imaginary student was no better than self-explaining (Lachner et al. 2021; *verified*). Teaching with real interaction showed larger gains for the explainer, but the evidence is only four studies (Kobayashi 2019; *unverified*).
- **H6.** People with more expertise predicted novices' task times worse, and debiasing did not fix this. People with intermediate expertise may predict best. Hinds 1999. *Minor issue.*
- **H7.** Three related results, all *minor issue*:
  - Clarification questions are the second most common code-review comment type. Reviewers new to the files say they look mainly for obvious bugs (Bacchelli & Bird 2013).
  - Perspective-based reading has no overall advantage, and review teams using it did worse than teams using checklists (Ciolkowski 2009).
  - Requirements inspectors with degrees outside computing found more defects (Carver et al. 2008).
- **H8.** Most risks found in architecture evaluations (57 of 99) were omissions (Bass et al. 2006; *unverified*). Inspection meetings added about 5% of defects in one study and 30% in another (Votta 1993; Porter et al. 1997; *verified, figure corrected*).
- **H9.** No controlled study compares rubber-duck debugging with not using a duck. *Minor issue.*

*Our reading:* ask how things work rather than why they were chosen, push toward deep questions, and use a separate questioner rather than "imagine a junior". Nothing here shows that noticing a gap leads to fixing it.

### LLM studies

- **L1.** Self-correction without outside feedback did not improve reasoning and often made it worse (Huang et al. 2024; Kamoi et al. 2024). Self-Refine's average gain of about 20% came mostly from tasks judged on preference and from constrained generation. Math gained 0.2 points (Madaan et al. 2023). *Minor issue.*
- **L2.** In Blocksworld, a GPT-4 plan verifier approved 84% of invalid plans. An external verifier raised accuracy from 40% to 88%; self-critique raised it only to 55%. Valmeekam et al. 2023. *Minor issue.*
- **L3.** Human feedback produced passing repairs 52.6% of the time, against 33.3% for GPT-4 feedback. Code Llama repaired 1.1% of programs, against 10.8% for GPT-4 (Olausson et al. 2024; *verified*). Explaining code without running tests gave small, uneven gains (Chen et al. 2023; *minor issue*).
- **L4.** Targeted questions were answered correctly about 70% of the time, against 17% for the same facts in long-form output. Answering without the draft in view did better. Chain-of-Verification, on fact-recall tasks. *Verified.*
- **L5.** Models picked the right question for a single missing fact only 40-50% of the time on planning and logic problems. QuestBench. *Verified.*
- **L6.** GPT-4 found about 53% of reasoning mistakes. Told where the error was, PaLM 2 recovered 18-44 points on wrong answers but lost up to 11 points on correct ones. Open models fixed errors presented as the user's far more often than the same errors presented as their own. Tyen et al. 2024; Tsui 2025. *Minor issue.*
- **L7.** Results on SWE-bench tasks with missing details:
  - Interaction raised resolve rates to 54-89% of what the models achieved with the full spec. Models rarely asked unless told to. Haiku 3.5 got the same information as Sonnet 3.5 but resolved 26.8% of tasks against 39.6% (Ambig-SWE; *minor issue*).
  - Haiku 3.5 was also worse at detecting missing details: 0.63, against 0.84-0.89 for Sonnet models (*verified*).
  - A separate agent that looks for gaps beat a single agent that decides for itself whether to ask: 69.4% against 61.2%, at $3.50 against $2.03 per task. A single agent told outright that the task was incomplete matched the separate agent (70.4% at $1.40) (Ask or Assume; *minor issue*).
  - In both studies, the simulated user held the true answers.
- **L8.** Asking and then refining raised code pass@1 by about 8 points for GPT-4 and ChatGPT. The answers came from a simulated owner of the requirements. ClarifyGPT. *Verified.*
- **L9.** A judge who could not see the source and heard one expert gained little: LLM judges went from 48% to 54% accuracy, humans from 60% to 78%. Persuasive experts who were wrong misled them. Debate did better. Khan et al. 2024. *Minor issue.*
- **L10.** When challenged, models changed 32-86% of their answers (Sharma et al. 2023). Across ten models, answers flipped 46% of the time (Laban et al. 2023). Models' explanations justified answers that were actually driven by biases they never mentioned (Turpin et al. 2023). *Verified.*
- **L11.** Novices simulated by a prompt alone act more competent than the role, and more so over several turns of dialogue (Xu et al. 2026, a workshop poster on simulated young learners; *minor issue*). Personas in system prompts had largely random effects on factual questions (Zheng et al. 2024; *verified*).
- **L12.** Scoring a teacher model's answers by how well weaker student models did on exams raised MATH accuracy by 3-6 points (Ning et al. 2024). Most of the gains credited to multi-agent debate come from voting. With the same number of samples, majority voting beat debate 88.2 to 83.0 (Choi et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2024). *Minor issue.*

*Our reading:* the best-supported mechanism is pointing to where the error is (L6) through narrow questions (L4), not explanation as such. The weakest link is trusting the author's answers (L1, L2, L9, L10).

### Agent systems and prior art

- **A1.** Architectures generated by five current models scored 0.54-0.67 F1 on components but only 0.09-0.18 F1 on the relationships between them (R2ABench, 17 requirements documents). GPT-3.5 sequence diagrams dropped conditions and hid conflicting requirements behind vague function calls (Ferrari et al. 2024). *Minor issue.*
- **A2.** In 1,642 multi-agent traces, the same failures recur: not asking for clarification, superficial checking, and stopping badly (MAST). Code models wrote code instead of asking in over 60% of ambiguous cases. A dedicated asking agent asked more often but also asked unneeded questions (HumanEvalComm). *Verified.*
- **A3.** GPT-3.5 agents were sometimes talked out of correct answers by another agent's wrong feedback. GPT-4 agents were not. AgentVerse. *Minor issue.*
- **A4.** ChatDev's questioning step raised a combined quality score from 0.309 to 0.395. This used GPT-3.5 and one run, and the agent asking was the one that would do the work. Separately, telling a reviewer the code was bug-free cut Claude 3.5 Haiku's vulnerability detection from 68% to 9%. *Unverified.*
- **A5.** This repo's R6 review found three things: plan quality rises with the planning model, a poor plan can be worse than no plan, and every planner-executor split that kept quality kept a strong model or a verifier in the loop. *Verified.*
- **A6.** In the local pilot, Sonnet reviewing and Haiku revising passed 6 of 12 steps, against 7 of 12 for Haiku alone. Cost per passing step was $0.281 against $0.077. *Not audited; rechecked against the local file.*
- **A7. Practitioner prior art:**
  - Matt Pocock's `grilling` skill works in rounds over a "frontier" of open decisions and finishes when nothing is left silently assumed. In grilling, the person answering is the human who owns the decisions.
  - The superpowers `writing-plans` skill.
  - Parnas's notes on active design reviews ("make the reviewers use the documents").

  None of these reports outcome data. *Unverified; anecdotal.*

### Evaluation methods and Claude Code mechanics

- **E1.** LLM judges favour the first answer shown, longer answers and answers from their own model family. Length alone moved one model's win rate anywhere from 23% to 64%. When grading against a rubric, judges can be more than 50% more likely to wrongly pass items their own output failed (Zheng et al. 2023; Dubois et al. 2024; Pombal et al. 2026; *verified*). Checklists graded one item at a time modestly raised agreement with humans (Cook et al. 2024; *minor issue*).
- **E2.** Several findings bear on eval design, all *unverified*:
  - Scores vary by 2-6 points from one run to the next.
  - Error bars computed per case can be three times wider than naive ones.
  - The choice of model playing the simulated user moved outcomes by up to 9 points.
  - Tuning a prompt on a development set overfit it by 5-20 points.
  - A fixed downstream model implementing each plan told different plan sources apart.
- **M1.** In this repo's activation eval, the code-comments skill loaded in 0 of 18 runs where it should have for Haiku, and 3 of 18 for Sonnet (*not audited; rechecked against the local file*). Vercel reports that a skill was never invoked in 56% of its cases (*unverified*).
- **M2.** The Claude Code docs say the following (*unverified*):
  - Plugin hooks fire whether or not a skill loads.
  - Stop hooks can block the agent from stopping, but they do not list which files were edited.
  - A plugin agent with no `tools` list inherits every tool.
  - `model` can be set per call.
  - A subagent can be resumed with SendMessage.
  - `claude plugin eval` compares runs with and without a plugin.

## 3. Risks and counterevidence

- **Made-up answers.** The junior cannot check anything. The author can justify a wrong answer fluently (L10), and a persuasive expert who is wrong misleads more than having no expert (L9). The plan may get longer and more confidently wrong, which is the poor plan that is worse than none (A5).
- **People-pleasing revisions.** Questions read as challenges. Models drop correct answers under challenge (L10, A3), and revising correct steps costs accuracy (L6).
- **Shallow questions and a junior who knows too much.** Novice questions are mostly shallow and get answered by restating the plan (H4). LLMs find the real gap about half the time (L5). Dedicated askers ask too much (A2). A strong model playing a junior fills gaps from its own knowledge instead of asking (L11).
- **The junior says "I understand" too early.** LLM verifiers approve flawed plans (L2), and agents handle stopping badly (A2).
- **Length bias and extra compute.** Revised plans are longer and are often graded by a judge from the same model family (E1). Many multi-agent gains come from extra samples or a better prompt (L7, L12).
- **Weak models using answers poorly, cost, and the skill not activating** (L7, A6, M1).

## 4. Design implications for Junior-dev

- **Trigger.**
  - *Evidence-based:* don't rely on the skill description alone. Haiku loaded the existing skill 0 of 18 times (M1), and hooks fire either way (M2).
  - *Our inference:* use two hooks. A PostToolUse(Write|Edit) hook writes the paths of plans and diagrams (Mermaid fences, plan headings) to a marker file. A Stop hook then requires one junior pass per marked plan, guarded by `stop_hook_active`. Test what the hooks actually receive as input before building on it.
- **Context isolation.**
  - *Evidence-based:* experts misjudge what novices will find hard (H6), and checkers do better when they are not anchored to the draft (L4).
  - *Our inference:* pass only the plan, remove any "this is complete" wording (A4), and give the junior an explicit, minimal tools list. Leaving the list out gives it repo access (M2).
- **Persona.**
  - *Evidence-based:* role-play alone does not make a model act like a novice (L11), so give the junior a procedure (A1, L6):
    - trace one request from end to end;
    - for each node and edge, ask what triggers it, what data moves, who owns the state and what happens on failure;
    - treat anything the plan does not state as unknown;
    - cite the node, edge or step each question is about.
  - *Our inference:* frame it as an intermediate, fluent in software design but new to this stack (H6, H7).
- **Batches and rounds.**
  - *Evidence-based:* require mostly deep questions (how, what-if, failure) and log each question's type (H3, H4). It is unclear how much later rounds add (H8).
  - *Our inference:* batches of 5-8 questions, at most 2-3 rounds, and stop as soon as a round changes nothing. Borrow grilling's frontier structure (A7).
- **Stop condition.**
  - *Evidence-based:* the junior saying "I understand" is not a reliable signal (L2, L9, A2).
  - *Our inference:* a fresh junior reads only the revised plan and walks through one or two concrete scenarios. The main agent marks every place where that walk-through differs from what it intended, and each mismatch becomes an edit or an open item. L12 and H1 support this indirectly.
- **Questions the main agent cannot answer truthfully.** *Evidence-based* (L1, L3, L7, L10): answer from repo evidence (a file, a symbol, command output) or mark the answer UNKNOWN or ASSUMPTION. Unknowns become open items or questions for the user, never guesses. Check the repo, not the plan text (L4).
- **Revise step.**
  - *Evidence-based* (L6, L10, A3): sort each question into one of four bins: already covered (say where), a gap, an error, or out of scope. Edit only for gaps and errors, with a one-line reason for each.
  - *Our inference:* everything learned has to end up in the plan itself, because the final check reads only the plan.
- **Junior model.** *Weak evidence:* Sonnet-class models detected gaps better than Haiku 3.5 (L7). That suggests a Sonnet junior even when Haiku is the main agent, though a cheaper junior may still work (L12). These results are on older models, so test it.

## 5. Recommended eval design

- **Cases.**
  - *Seeded gaps:* start from a correct plan or Mermaid diagram with a hidden list of 8-15 details it must contain. Delete or corrupt 2-4 of them. Label each one as incomplete, inconsistent, ambiguous or architectural (a missing link, a reversed flow, the wrong layer, a missing failure path) (A1, A2).
  - *Null:* complete plans where little should change.
  - *Natural:* Haiku or Sonnet writes its own plan for a task, scored against a hidden list fixed in advance.
  - *Activation:* prompts that produce a plan and prompts that don't.
- **Arms.**
  - *NoReview*.
  - *SelfReview*: the main agent reviews its own plan, with the same token budget.
  - *JuniorPrompt*: one line, "write it so a junior new to this stack could follow".
  - *Critic*: a reviewer that sees only the plan.
  - *Junior*: the treatment, seeing only the plan.
  - *JuniorFull*: the junior also sees the repo and the conversation.
  - *Oracle*: the main agent is told which items are missing (the ceiling).

  The comparisons that decide it:
  - Junior against SelfReview and JuniorPrompt: is it the loop, or just the instruction?
  - Junior against Critic: does the novice framing matter?
  - Junior against JuniorFull: does isolation matter?

  Beating NoReview alone proves nothing (L7, L12).
- **Metrics.**
  - For each seeded gap, record three things: did a question touch it, was the answer correct, was the plan fixed. Report the share of gaps fixed and the share of cases where every gap was fixed with nothing broken.
  - Safety checks: harmful edits, invented facts, changes to null cases, saying "understood" too early, length, and cost per gap fixed.
  - Confirmation on a subset: a fixed model implements each arm's plan against hidden tests. Use cases where that model passes 25-75% of the time without help (E2).
- **Grading.**
  - Mechanical checks come first: the Mermaid parses, and the required nodes and links are present.
  - Then one yes/no judge per hidden item. The judge comes from a non-Claude model family or a mix of families. It does not know which arm it is grading, sees no transcripts, and is calibrated against human labels.
  - An overall preference judgment is only a secondary measure, run in both orders and adjusted for length (E1).
  - Before any paid runs, test the grader. The correct plan must pass. The seeded plan must fail exactly the seeded items. Padding must earn nothing, and answers given only in chat must earn nothing.
- **Models.** Haiku and Sonnet as main agents (Opus as a ceiling if affordable), crossed with Haiku and Sonnet juniors. Add one other junior model as a check on how much the results depend on the junior (E2).
- **Sample size.** Pilot with 12-20 cases (4-6 per defect type plus null cases), 3 runs each. That size can only detect effects of about 15-20 points in the share of cases fully fixed. Compare arms within each case, compute error bars over cases, and fix in advance both the main comparison (Junior minus SelfReview) and the size of difference that would count (E2).
- **Cost drivers.**
  - The number of rounds, questions per batch and answer length.
  - Answering from the repo.
  - The number of arms.
  - The downstream implementation runs.
  - Judges from other model families.

  A separate questioning agent cost about 1.7 times a single agent that decided for itself (L7). Price a small trial run first and set a cost ceiling in advance.
- **Hill-climbing loop.**
  1. Split cases about 60/40 into a development set and a held-out set, keeping each case family on one side.
  2. Change one thing per round: the question procedure, the batch size, the stop rule or the revision rules.
  3. Run 3 times per arm and read the transcripts.
  4. Keep a change only if Junior minus SelfReview improves and no safety check gets worse.
  5. Stop when scores plateau, and use the held-out set only at milestones (E2).

  Tune the trigger wording separately, in the activation eval.

## 6. Open questions

**Only you can answer:**
- Is success a plan that reads more completely, or better implementations? That choice sets the main measure.
- May the main agent check the repo, or ask you, while answering? Grounding answers in a real source is the best-supported lever.
- How much extra cost per plan is acceptable, and should Junior-dev run on every plan or only on architectural ones?
- If a critic beats Junior-dev at the same cost, is the novice framing still a requirement?
- Who writes the hidden answer lists, separately from whoever writes the skill?

**The eval must answer:**
- Does Junior-dev beat SelfReview and JuniorPrompt, for Haiku and for Sonnet? Do weaker authors benefit more, or do they make poor use of the answers?
- Which defect types do the junior's questions catch? How often are answers made up, with and without the repo to check against?
- Does isolation beat full context? Does the scenario walk-through track whether the plan is complete better than "I understand" does?
- Which junior model and how many rounds give the best value? Do the gains carry over to plans the agent writes itself, and to the implementations?
- Do the hooks fire in headless and plugin-eval runs? Does SendMessage work under `claude -p`? Can a subagent be given no tools at all?
